\subsection{Key Variables}

\autoref{fig:efficiency_distribution} reveals that there cannot be a unique criterion for quality based on efficiency given the rapid improvement of the industry. If we were to set an arbitrary efficiency threshold for quality at 20\% we would be considering in 2011 close to 0 panels able to reach this level of efficiency. Therefore, we define the premium nature of the panel given its place in the distribution. 

From here we can build a first set of quality criteria based on the \textit{efficiency} of the solar panels model, but also on the presence of additional features such as an integrated micro-inverter which contributes to the overall quality of the product. 

\paragraph{Quality Metrics 1} We described as \textit{premium panels}, panels whose efficiency is in the top 5\% of the efficiency distribution for the year preceding the implementation of the tariff. This is a dummy variable.

\vspace{1em}
This approach has already been used in past work on this database \citep{nemetWhatFactorsAffect2016, oshaughnessyNonmonotonicEffectsMarket2019}. However, to reinforce our empirical approach and embrace the various notions of quality that guide consumer choices we adopt the following alternative metrics. 

\paragraph{Quality Metrics 2} We define as \textit{Premium Installations} the installation that possesses not only \textit{premium panels} but also integrated micro-inverter and/or DC optimizer. This is a dummy variable.

\vspace{1em}
Integrated micro-inverters and DC optimizers contribute to the overall quality of solar panel installations but also increase the upfront cost of such installation. Therefore, including them as an additional quality criterion also extends the scope of our quality analysis.


\subsection{Good homogeneity}

In coherence with our model, we want to test whether the solar panel market can be described as a homogeneous good market or not. The first way to test is by regressing the price on quality and see if there is a premium for quality. If \autoref{fig:efficiency_distribution} provides indirect evidence of quality scope, the regression provides a consumer-view perspective on the taste for quality. In this logic, higher quality products would be more expansive, which is also motivated by \eqref{eq:price_quality_equation}. 

\begin{equation}
    \ln p_{i} = \beta_1Quality + \beta_2 Country \times Quality+\delta_s + \delta_t + \delta_i + \delta_m + \varepsilon_i 
\end{equation}

where $p_i$ is the pre-incentive price (\$/W), $Quality$ being either \textit{premium panels} or \textit{premium installations}, $Country$ represent the country of origin of the brand\footnote{We do not observe the manufacturing place of each panel, here country refers to the branding of the firm as being "American" because of its HQ location, even though the production may be in Philippine or Malaysia.}, and fixed effects for state ($s$), year ($t$), the installer $i$, and the manufacturer ($m$). The standard errors are clustered at zip code level.

\autoref{tab:price_quality_1} provides evidence of the presence of a small premium for quality type 1 panels in both HO and TPO systems. The presence of fixed effects, however, plays an important role in our estimation strategy. Given the fast pace at which prices declined during the decade (\textbf{see Figure}) and state demand shock associated with a possible change in subsidy policy or local economic boom or bust, adding these fixed effects reduces by two the estimated effects of quality on price. The negative overall effects of quality on price are probably because of Chinese, Korean, and American brands, which account for more than 70\% of the market shares, and panel varieties which absorb all the positive variation due to quality. We see that our quality premiums are strong and significant for Korean and American brands, which also correspond to qualitative insights from industry professionals \citep{Compare2025sBest2024}. It oscillates between 7.4\% and 4.8\% for Korean brands, and 4\% to 6.8\% for American brands depending on the inclusion of installer fixed effects, and is not statistically different from 0 for Chinese brands. 

When turning to the TPO systems we find very strong evidence of premium pricing though only statistically significant for Chinese brands, around 20\%. This surprising result might indicate that Chinese brands might be more distributed among third-party owner companies \textbf{see reg}. If we are to believe this correlation, this last result is coherent with the fact that homeowners not buying through intermediary providers do not provide a premium to Chinese panels, which can then be captured by TPO providers. This last result is in line with both qualitative \citep{pyperDeepPoliticsDark2016} and academic evidence \citep{plessPassThroughTestMarket2019}.

However, these descriptive results have a few caveat. The first is that for HO system once we introduce a fixed effect for installer, even though our estimate are individually significant, we massively reject the joint significance with the F-test. All the variation seems to be absorbed by the installer fixed effects, which reflects both the great dispersion in prices but also of installation. If very few firms produce 90\% of the panel on the market, there are more than 5800 installers. The distribution of installation is very different in the two systems types (see \autoref{fig:distrib_installer}). If the mean installation per installer is the same for both system, we can see in \autoref{fig:distrib_installer} that the distribution for TPO system is much more narrow and concentrated among large actors while for HO system, if there are big actors, overall the installation system is more spread. This reflect two logic of buying. One with an installer buying large quantity of (Chinese) solar panels largely passing the cost onto consumers, and the other consumers buying panels and contracting with local installers. Therefore, it considerably reduces the variation once it is captures by the fixed effects for HO systems. Conversely, if we do not observe a similar result for TPO system it is probably because fewer installer assure a greater share of the market. Finally, the Wald-test are significative in all configurations which reassures us on the presence of quality markups.

The second, is that we consider a threshold of 20\% of efficiency over the period for this first quality metric, which represent top 5\% efficiency only after 2013. It covers 7 out of 10 years of our sample but is a limitation of our analysis for the first year of the period considered. Nonetheless, the number of installations quickly increased after 2013\footnote{90\% of the observations are after 2013}, making it a negligible issue here. For completeness, we included a period relative quality metrics following our definition (\textbf{see} ).

Our second quality metrics bring roughly similar estimates of quality premium for Chinese, Korean and American brands for HO systems with the full set of fixed effects. Chinese and American brands benefit of 6\% and 3.5\% premium on prices, but Korean brands are now not statistically not different from 0. Moreover, we can notice that premium are estimated to be much higher overall with this configuration, being 2 digits in most configurations. This even more striking when shifting to TPO systems. All firms have large premium markups, close to or above 20\%. This might reflect two things with respect to our first quality metrics. First, we include more panels than just the one with highest module efficiency of the distribution, less efficient panels but with premium features (i.e. built-in micro inverter) are sold for a relatively higher price as they improve the overall efficiency of the installation. For HO system it reflects with slightly higher premium than in \autoref{tab:price_quality_1}. But for TPO systems, it reflects very high markups, meaning they are probably able to bargain more with their customers or by default install more complete systems to pass-on more costs. If those results seems high, they are in line with other estimations such as \citet{barboseTrackingSunPricing2019a}.

\begin{itemize}

    \item New models being proposed to the market $\longrightarrow$ more efficient and/or more pricy
    \item Persistence of some models on the market: what are the characteristics of models that stay on the market, those with the lowest price elasticity, the highest quality? (Kaplan-Meier, Cox Model), market share by segment of the market.
