\subsubsection{Tracking the Sun}
Our main data set is \textit{Tracking the Sun} (hereafter TTS), published by the Berkeley Lawrence Lab with publicly collected data across the U.S. It is an extensive database considering the universe of installed solar panels in the U.S. with detailed information about the localization, size, total price of installation, rebate\footnote{Excluding the Federal ITC.}, module manufacturer, installer, presence of integrated micro inverter, the technology used, the efficiency, and the presence of battery. 

This comprehensive dataset represents 95\% of all installed systems in our period and 56\% of the overall U.S. installed capacity \citep{houdeIncidenceUSChinaSolar2023}. It includes only the system installed and connected to the U.S. grid, whether mounted on the roof or on the ground, for residential, community or small-scale utility use. It excludes all systems above 1MW of installed capacity, which corresponds to a large utility-scale solar system. A classical home installation is around 300kW.

\paragraph{Cleaning Procedure} The first step of our cleaning procedure has been to clean the dataset of misspelled names of module manufacturers. The brands more largely misspelled are also among the biggest, among which are Hanwha Qcells, Canadian Solar, Panasonic, and Solar Power\footnote{Respectively South Korean, Chinese, Japanese and Chinese.}. This allowed us to retrieve thousands of installed systems otherwise lost.

After that, we deflated the prices of installed solar systems and the rebate provided by the CPI value we obtained from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. We took the first year of the period we consider, 2010, as a reference year. 

We then merged our data with the \textit{zipcode} which matches zip code to county, and provided information on the real estate market from the 2010 American Community Survey conducted by the Census Bureau.

Interestingly, \autoref{fig:within_firms_quality} reveals that for the periods of the anti-dumping tariffs (2010-2012, 2013-2016) we can see an increase in the average level of product delivered, but very small vertical differentiation of product. The third tariff moment introduced is correlated with greater vertical differentiation between and within firms that have introduced more differentiated products. Along with \autoref{fig:efficiency_distribution} we have some descriptive proof of the effect of tariff on the quality scope of available protected in the protected markets. Indeed, the model predicts that an increase in tariff would decrease the scope for quality by reducing the market access to higher-quality goods. Given the fact the solar market in the U.S. is largely composed of imports, this descriptive effect of tariffs would not be surprising. All exporters to the U.S. need to shift up the quality to maintain themselves on the market like \autoref{fig:efficiency_distribution_subd} seems to show.
